I. The Great Divergence — and the original sin of the rating industry

The twentieth century gave us two master metrics. GDP measured what an economy produces. The credit rating measured whether a borrower repays. Both became load-bearing: entire institutions exist to make those numbers move.

The twenty-first century's defining question is different: can a civilization execute the future it has announced?

Humanity has mapped the capital required for the transition — ~$42.7 trillion across twenty sectors — and has announced stupendous fractions of it. Announced capital is loud: it arrives with press releases, summits, and head-of-state stagecraft. Commissioned capital is quiet: it arrives with grid-connection certificates, commissioning reports, and steel in the ground. Because the first is loud and the second is quiet, the world's information systems systematically over-record intent and under-record reality. A chasm has opened between announced intent and commissioned reality, and the instruments that should measure the chasm don't.

ESG — the incumbent paradigm — began as a serious attempt and degenerated into a compliance machine: it scores policies, disclosures, and promises. It announces that an announcement was announced. It cannot tell a minister, an allocator, or a schoolchild whether the money will become steel in the ground.

This is the original sin of the whole class of ratings: the measurement of intent, sold as a proxy for reality.

This document declares the correction:

Execution is the only valid metric of intent.

The TFCI is not an ESG index. It is the first instrument of transition physics: it measures the structural capacity of a jurisdiction to metabolize capital into delivered infrastructure — and it submits its own measurements to falsification, in public, on a schedule. We measure credibility — the demonstrated, historical, physical capacity to convert announcements into delivered reality — not reputation, not policy, not wealth.

Two quantities, one instrument. Every number the TFCI publishes ultimately rests on two ledgers: what a jurisdiction announced, and what it commissioned. The distance between them — the Credibility Gap — is the instrument's defining measurement. It is computed from an append-only, event-sourced record of every announcement, every revision, every financial investment decision, every commissioning, and every cancellation. The ledger cannot be rewritten, and therefore neither can history. Where the evidence is still thin, the instrument does not guess and does not hide: it publishes the coverage state of its own knowledge. An index that measures execution must, above all, execute honestly.

II. Four syntheses

A paradigm shift does not aggregate knowledge; it finds hidden structural isomorphisms between distant fields and fuses them. Four such fusions ground this Index. Each opens with its lineage.

1. Thermodynamic Capital Theory

Thermodynamics × cellular biology × macroeconomics.

Lineage. The application of thermodynamics to economics descends from Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen (The Entropy Law and the Economic Process, 1971) through Robert Ayres and Herman Daly; the city-as-organism lineage from Abel Wolman's urban metabolism (1965).

The TFCI delta. Those frameworks used thermodynamics to explain macro-level growth and entropy. The TFCI diverges by applying a micro-biological metabolic model to jurisdictional execution: financial capital is treated not as abstract energy but as ATP, which becomes toxic (stranded) if the jurisdiction lacks the structural organelles — the five pillars — to metabolize it. We do not measure the entropy of GDP; we measure the cellular metabolism of capital. A Physical Bandwidth veto is apoptosis: the sector dies because the substrate is absent. The TFCI is the first framework to measure the thermodynamic efficiency of capital conversion rather than the eloquence of its announcement.

2. Topological Veto Theory

Graph theory × Kant × materials science.

Lineage. The weakest-link insight descends from Eliyahu Goldratt's Theory of Constraints (1984) and, far older, Justus von Liebig's Law of the Minimum (1840); network cascades in economics from Acemoglu and colleagues. The doctrine of an imperative that cannot be traded off descends from Kant.

The TFCI delta. Goldratt applied the limiting factor to factory throughput. The TFCI elevates it from a production bottleneck to a Topological Veto: in sovereign execution, a missing pillar is not friction to be optimized but a topological rupture in the execution graph — the sector is mathematically deleted, and no quantity of regulatory elegance or capital depth bridges a topological void. Two trigger paths (a pillar below the floor; a documented blocking event), one consequence: non-compensation, at whatever average the remaining pillars reach. Transition execution obeys the law of the minimum, not the law of averages.

3. Cybernetic Accountability — trust with memory

Cybernetics × control theory × behavioral economics.

Lineage. The feedback loop is Norbert Wiener's (Cybernetics, 1948); the idea that a system's state depends on its history descends from materials physics and was carried into macroeconomics by Blanchard and Summers (1986) and the persistence debates they opened.

The TFCI delta. We adapt hysteresis into Algorithmic Credibility Hysteresis: trust is lost instantly and rebuilt slowly — zero recovery in the first quarter after a veto resolves, capped at +4 points per quarter thereafter. The Conversion Ledger is the causal engine that closes the loop: every announcement opens an account, every commissioning closes it, every cancellation is recorded as the falsification signal it is, and the Credibility Gap is the delta between the two sums. The Index enforces the temporal reality of trust arithmetically — and applies the same memory to itself, publishing its own errors on a schedule.

4. Ontological Friction

Phenomenology × materials science × institutional economics.

Lineage. Friction-as-cost descends from Coase (The Nature of the Firm, 1937) and Williamson; the lifeworld from Husserl and Schutz; the technology-in-the-lifeworld lineage from Latour and Ihde.

The TFCI delta. Transaction costs are financial and legal; they can be paid. Ontological Friction is physical and existential: the thermodynamic resistance generated when abstract financial capital collides with the messy reality of a specific geography — water scarcity, legacy drag, NIMBYism, consent. It converts the kinetic energy of capital into the waste heat of stranded assets. Unlike transaction costs, ontological friction cannot be bought away; it can only be measured. The Legacy Drag and Social License pillars price it — the first framework to do so at sovereign scale.

IV. The charter — five commitments

Execution over intent. We score what a jurisdiction can deliver, never what it announces.

Physics is non-negotiable. No capital compensates for an absent condition of possibility.

Falsifiability. Scores are predictions; the Conversion Ledger — append-only, event-sourced, publicly accountable — tests them. The Gap is computed from that ledger, published on the instrument's landing page, and backtested annually. The Index measures itself with the same instruments it applies to others, and where its evidence is incomplete it publishes the coverage state rather than a silence.

Universality. One rulebook, from Norway to Nauru — the veil test written in schema. Where evidence runs thin, the rulebook does not bend; the evidence regime is published instead. Every score carries its provenance: analyst, hybrid, or empirical.

Memory. Trust degrades instantly and recovers slowly; the Index's own history is part of its body, and its ledger is immutable by construction.

V. The succession

GDP was never meant to carry the weight it carries. Its own architect, Simon Kuznets, warned the United States Congress in 1934 against mistaking the measure of production for the measurement of purpose; the world spent ninety years learning that he was right — and reasons to build the successor instrument anyway.

In 1997, the world gathered in Kyoto and declared the destination. The Kyoto Protocol was the triumph of intent: for the first time, the future of the physical planet was written into a framework of commitment. The era that followed — the pledges, the targets, the summits — produced declarations in abundance. What it never produced was an instrument capable of answering the one question that matters to every one of those commitments: will it be built?

This document is written in Kyoto, in the spirit of what Kyoto declared, to supply what the era lacked. Not a new protocol. Not a new target. An instrument: a falsifiable rulebook by which the distance between the announced and the delivered is measured, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, sector by sector, quarter by quarter. Intent thirty years ago; execution now. If the Protocol gave the century its destination, let this century's instrument be the gauge on its road.

We are moving from the Age of Rhetoric to the Age of Execution. The TFCI does not claim to be the destination. It claims to be the compass — fixed by rules, corrected by physics, audited by history.
